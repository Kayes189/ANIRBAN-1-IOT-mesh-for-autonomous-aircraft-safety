2  Executive Summary

ANIRBAN-1 proposes a cooperative, advisory-only aircraft safety overlay. Participating aircraft would exchange state and planned-trajectory intent, predict potential conflicts over a declared horizon, evaluate candidate resolutions against explicit constraints, and present an explainable recommendation to a pilot or operator. Existing collision-avoidance and air-traffic functions remain independent and unmodified throughout.

The founding research question is answerable, and the answer is currently "unproven but testable." The question — whether a carefully engineered, fault-aware cooperative trajectory-sharing and conflict-prediction layer can demonstrate measurable benefits beyond existing surveillance, collision-avoidance and trajectory-management approaches — can be tested on bench computers and simulators at low cost. However, no public evidence establishes that such a layer already exceeds the ACAS X / DAIDALUS / trajectory-based-operations class of systems. The defensible research frontier is narrower than the original concept assumed: not smarter avoidance logic, and not a new network, but whether aircraft can share intent and coordinate under fault, uncertainty and disagreement without ever making their existing safety depend on that cooperation.

Six corrections to the original concept pitch are carried through every subsequent section:

  1. TCAS is not a last-second panic button. ACAS II / TCAS II operates independently of ground ATC and issues Traffic and vertical Resolution Advisories. It is a complement ANIRBAN-1 must never contradict.
  2. Ground radar is not obsolete. ADS-B complements surveillance with roughly one-second GNSS-derived broadcasts, but visibility depends on equipage, coverage and reception, and the broadcast carries no native authentication.
  3. 6G is not an available aviation safety network. IMT-2030 performance requirements were drafted in February 2026 with commercial framing around 2030. A dedicated 6G air-to-air mesh is future research, not a prototype dependency.
  4. Consensus and ledgers cannot establish sensor truth. Authentication proves message origin and integrity only. General-purpose blockchain consensus is removed from the time-critical loop; coordination uses a deterministic, timeout-bounded proposal/acknowledgement protocol with declared disagreement outcomes.
  5. LiDAR, cameras and radar are not universal detectors. Airborne small-target detection is a bounded research question requiring measured probability-of-detection and false-alarm characteristics against a declared target envelope.
  6. The mesh itself is not the invention. IEEE 1920.1 already defines infrastructure-free air-to-air ad hoc networking. ANIRBAN-1 should build on such services and position its contribution above the transport layer.

The recommended Version 0.1 is defined by ten system requirements (SYS-001 to SYS-010, Table 4), two of which are hard gates: no flight-control output (SYS-007) and fail-closed suppression of advice when inputs are inadequate (SYS-006). Validation proceeds through stages V0–V6 with declared exit evidence, benchmarked against three baselines and using ablation runs that disable one feature at a time so that any measured improvement is attributed rather than asserted. Novelty is stated strictly as four hypotheses (C1–C4, Table 22), each paired with the evidence it would require.

Numbers that could not be verified in the supplied sources — per-aircraft cost bands, power draw, fuel-burn impact, communication uptime targets, alert lead-time figures, certification timelines, and assigned assurance levels — are recorded as unverified or removed rather than replaced with invented estimates. Section 15 lists every such decision.

3  Introduction, Motivation, Problem Statement, and Research Question

3.1 Motivation and industry context

The motivation for a cooperative layer is that tactical separation today is delivered by systems designed for a different problem. Onboard collision avoidance acts close to an encounter and normally constrains vertical manoeuvres; surveillance broadcast publishes current state, not a negotiated future path; and air-traffic services coordinate strategically through ground-mediated processes. A peer-to-peer exchange of intent is the missing element, and the commercial aviation connectivity market is often cited as evidence that the enabling infrastructure is maturing. Figure 1 shows that market framing, and the caveat matters more than the curve.

Interpretation limit. Market size is not a technical validation metric. The reported growth concentrates in predictive maintenance, asset tracking and cabin services; the same industry surveys identify cybersecurity, functional safety and sector conservatism as the three adoption challenges. A safety-adjacent collision function sits at the hardest certification tier of exactly those three challenges. Figure 1 therefore supports a statement about connectivity maturity only, and is retained here for context rather than as evidence that ANIRBAN-1 would work.

3.2 What existing collision avoidance already achieves

Any claim about an additional safety layer must be measured against the systems that already exist. The most important reference point is the successor to TCAS II. The ACAS X development line replaces fixed rule tables with Markov-decision-process logic and machine-learning-optimized lookup tables, supports vertical, horizontal and blended resolution advisories, and fuses ADS-B In data. Programme-reported results attribute a 65 per cent reduction in unnecessary alerts and a 20 per cent reduction in mid-air collision risk relative to TCAS II, as shown in Figure 2.

The implication for ANIRBAN-1 is uncomfortable but clarifying. Optimized, sensor-flexible, probabilistic collision avoidance with multi-directional advisories is a funded, standardized, legislatively advancing programme: European legislation already accepts configurations requiring aircraft above defined seat or weight thresholds to equip ACAS Xa or ACAS II 7.1. ANIRBAN-1 novelty therefore cannot rest on smarter avoidance logic. What remains open is the peer-to-peer fault semantics above it — and Figure 2 sets the bar against which any ANIRBAN-1 alerting claim must be compared, not surpassed by assertion.

3.3 Related technology: trajectory-based operations

Sharing a future path is not itself unprecedented. Trajectory-based operations (TBO) share and dynamically maintain a four-dimensional trajectory among air traffic control, airlines and aircraft, with the flight management system managing speed against required arrival times. National programmes have moved this from paper to flight. Figure 3 reports the contents of one such verification.

Figure 3 establishes that minutes-horizon trajectory sharing between aircraft and ground services is being implemented by state programmes. It does not establish anything about ANIRBAN-1. A two-aircraft verification does not demonstrate safety case adequacy, scalability to dense traffic, certification readiness, or the effectiveness of a different architecture. Its relevance to this report is narrow and specific: it confirms that the intent-sharing premise rests on live groundwork, which shifts ANIRBAN-1 contribution away from the trajectory mathematics and toward what those programmes do not yet supply — aircraft-to-aircraft intent exchange with declared fault semantics between peers, without waiting for ground-mediated agreement.

3.4 Problem statement and research question

The problem ANIRBAN-1 addresses is therefore not the absence of collision avoidance, surveillance or trajectory management. It is the absence of a documented, testable specification for peer coordination under fault: what an aircraft does when a peer’s intent message is stale, missing, contradictory, expired, or adversarial; what happens when the network partitions; and how a human is told that the system no longer knows enough to advise.

The research question is stated for testing, not for advocacy:

Research question. Can a carefully engineered, fault-aware cooperative trajectory-sharing and conflict-prediction layer demonstrate measurable benefits beyond existing aviation surveillance, collision avoidance, and trajectory-management approaches — where "benefits" are defined by pre-declared metrics, measured against named baselines, and attributed by ablation?

This report answers the question honestly: at concept stage the answer is that the question is testable and the testbed is inexpensive, and that the benefit remains undemonstrated. Sections 12 and 14 define what would constitute an acceptable answer.

4  System Scope, Objectives, Assumptions, and Non-Goals

4.1 Version 0.1 scope

Version 0.1 operates primarily in simulation. It processes simulated or appropriately available aircraft-state and traffic information; maintains a local representation of nearby traffic with declared uncertainty; predicts potential conflicts over a declared prediction horizon; evaluates alternative trajectories under explicit constraints; exchanges simulated aircraft intent and candidate proposals; handles stale messages, communication loss, disagreement and invalid inputs; presents recommendations through a research interface; and remains isolated from flight-control commands, leaving existing ACAS/TCAS functions and applicable operational procedures independent.

It does not begin by assuming that aircraft automatically execute maneuvers negotiated by an algorithm. Expansion paths — hardware-in-the-loop testing, possible approved aircraft integration, additional sensors, new communication services, eventual certification — are described separately in Section 13, each with its own prerequisites and exit criteria.

4.2 Explicit non-goals

Six non-goals define the evidentiary boundary of Version 0.1. Each removes a burden of proof the programme cannot currently meet, and each is paired with a future expansion path that carries its own prerequisites (Section 13).

  • Replace TCAS/ACAS, ADS-B, radar, ATC, the flight management system, or operating procedures.
  • Automatically steer, climb, descend, slow, or send commands to an autopilot or flight-control computer.
  • Assume all aircraft carry ANIRBAN equipment or share complete future four-dimensional trajectories.
  • Claim that LiDAR or cameras see all birds, drones, balloons, gliders, or non-cooperative aircraft.
  • Require live 6G, cloud connectivity, or blockchain to detect a local conflict.
  • Claim certification compliance on the basis of this concept document.

The second non-goal is decisive. Once the system has no write path to flight control, its worst failure class changes from an aircraft-level safety event to misleading information, which is what permits bench-computer development at research pace. The sixth pre-empts the most common failure mode of concept documents of this kind: presenting a list of standard names as if it were an assurance argument.

4.3 Standing assumptions

  • Unequipped traffic exists. A portion of nearby traffic will never participate; the local picture must remain usable with cooperative data only.
  • Intent is a claim. A received intended trajectory is a statement about planned behaviour, not a guarantee of future position.
  • Networks fail. Loss, delay, reordering, duplication and partition are normal conditions to be designed for, not anomalies.
  • Authentication is not truth. A correctly signed message may still carry an incorrect navigation solution, a wrong clock, or a compromised sender.
  • Access is permission-bound. Direct access to flight-management or collision-avoidance internals is aircraft-specific and must never be assumed.
  • A criterion must be declared. Every conflict result depends on the chosen separation or well-clear criterion and its operating domain.

Its organising principle is evidential hygiene: nothing reaches the prediction path without a timestamp, a source identity, a validity flag, and an uncertainty representation or an explicitly declared unknown. 6  Surveillance Inputs, Sensors, and Non-Cooperative Object Detection

6.1 Cooperative surveillance: what ADS-B provides and what it does not

ADS-B broadcasts GNSS-derived aircraft state automatically. ADS-B Out transmits position, velocity and identity about once per second over 1090ES or 978 MHz UAT; ADS-B In receives traffic from other aircraft and ground services, adding traffic information, flight information and relay services. Compared with conventional secondary surveillance radar, reported figures give ADS-B a 0.5-second update interval against a 4–12 second radar band, and roughly 30 metres of position accuracy against roughly 400 metres. Figures 6 and 7 present those two comparisons.

Figures 6 and 7 can establish that ADS-B offers more frequent and more precise cooperative state than conventional secondary radar. They cannot establish anything about ANIRBAN-1 performance. Four properties are frequently conflated and are kept separate throughout this report:

  • Update frequency — how often state is refreshed (Figure 6).
  • Positional accuracy — how close a reported position is to truth (Figure 7).
  • Authentication and integrity — whether the receiver can establish origin and immutability. ADS-B was designed without security mechanisms and is susceptible to message injection and sequencing manipulation; neither figure addresses this at all.
  • Traffic coverage and intent — ADS-B sees transponder-equipped aircraft only, and it publishes current state, not a negotiated future four-dimensional trajectory. This is precisely the gap the ANIRBAN-1 intent layer proposes to fill.

ANIRBAN-1 therefore inherits both the coverage boundary and the integrity gap of its cooperative feed. Every ADS-B-derived track enters the system with age, source and quality flags (SYS-002) and is treated as a time-bounded claim to be cross-checked, not as ground truth.

6.2 Non-cooperative sensing: four problems, not one

The supplied concept documents are internally inconsistent about sensor performance, and the inconsistency is recorded here rather than averaged away. The concept body describes a forward-scanning LiDAR at 100–300 metres, depth cameras effective to roughly 1–2 km in clear conditions, and small-target detection radars at roughly 5–10 km; the comparison table at the end of the same document lists LiDAR at 0.5–1 km and cameras at 1–5 km. These ranges differ by multiples within one document. They are carried as unverified design assumptions requiring measurement, not as specifications.

The detect-and-avoid literature is explicit that no single modality covers the threat set, and that fusion does not automatically produce reliable detection. ACAS Xu was designed to accept flexible sensor inputs including radar so that advice can degrade gracefully when non-cooperative sensing is absent; DAIDALUS supports multi-sensor input through configuration rather than claiming universal coverage.

Detection, identification, tracking and threat assessment are four distinct problems with four distinct error distributions. Published human-in-the-loop research makes the gap concrete: pilots lost well-clear against non-cooperative intruders significantly more often than against cooperative ones, driven by the radar’s declared range and sensor noise — that is, by the distance between an instantaneous detection and a usable track with bounded uncertainty. The same body of work records that the format of an alert changes pilot response behaviour and requires dedicated training. ANIRBAN-1 therefore maintains vocabulary discipline: a LiDAR point is not a track; a track is not an identification; an identification is not a threat assessment; and a threat assessment is not a validated avoidance recommendation.

6.3 Recommended initial sensing configuration

The smallest sensible initial configuration keeps sensing entirely out of the live loop. The optional sensor module consumes recorded imagery or simulated targets, produces bounded target tracks with explicit confidence, and reports a measured detection and false-alarm distribution against a declared target envelope defined by size class, closure rate, aspect and weather categories.

Features postponed until a separate study justifies them: airborne LiDAR hardware selection; nose or belly fairing mounting with de-icing provisions, which are airframe-specific engineering decisions rather than concept-level ones; weather-radar-adjacent modes; and any universal detection claim. This report states plainly that nothing in the current evidence base supports asserting that a sensor suite detects every bird, balloon, drone, glider or non-cooperative aircraft, and ANIRBAN-1 makes no such claim. Published assessments also characterize the certification basis for non-cooperative sensors as a current gap, which limits beyond-visual-line-of-sight approval in mixed airspace.

7  Communications, Message Formats, Coordination, and Failure Behaviour

7.1 Link options compared

The original concept treated an aircraft mesh as an open frontier and a dedicated 6G network as an eventual substrate. The evidence requires both framings to be revised: IEEE 1920.1 defines infrastructure-free air-to-air ad hoc networking for civil and commercial manned and unmanned aircraft, with a network services architecture, a security framework and data models, published as a draft standard in October 2022. The design consequence is to stop inventing a transport layer and to place the research contribution above it.

The safety lesson from airborne-network research is consistent across three strands of published work: backbone-style architectures that keep a connected three-dimensional corridor; aviation ad hoc networks supporting direct, relay and opportunistic transmission to extend air-to-ground access into oceanic and remote airspace; and connectivity-aware mobility models that trade area coverage against network connectivity. With nodes moving at hundreds of kilometres per hour, link intermittency and message age — not bandwidth — are the governing constraints. Message age is therefore a first-class protocol field, every peer interaction has a declared deadline, and the safety logic remains functional when the multi-hop path fails.

What the message format does not guarantee. The integrity and authentication metadata field, when populated with a working scheme, establishes message origin and in-flight immutability. It does not establish that the sender’s navigation solution is honest, that its clock is synchronized, or that its declared intent will be flown. The schema’s answer to that limit is the uncertainty and source-quality fields combined with expiry: every receiver treats message content as a time-bounded claim subject to plausibility and cross-source checks. Authentication and message integrity must not be confused with verification that the underlying sensor information is truthful.

7.3 Coordination: deterministic negotiation, not consensus

7.4 Loss, partition, disagreement and degradation

No aircraft may hold its local conflict detection, its existing safety functions, or its degraded-mode display in reserve behind a global-consensus step. Coordination is enrichment, never a dependency. Resilience design starts from published network behaviour rather than optimism: predictive tooling can forecast data-link interruption minutes ahead, which is useful for strategic deconfliction and useless for an encounter that develops inside a minute; and complex-network vulnerability analysis of aviation systems shows that targeted attacks degrade such networks far more severely than random failures, meaning an intentional adversary will seek the connectivity-critical nodes — relay positions, timing sources and certificate services. 8.3 Deterministic core with machine learning at arm’s length

Four technology routes were compared before selection. Deterministic algorithms — constant-velocity extrapolation and geometric separation tests — are predictable and easy to test, but under-report uncertainty and miss manoeuvring traffic. Optimization methods, such as polynomial trajectory families minimizing deviation cost, produce explicit trade-off logic and reproducible optima but grow combinatorially with participant count. Rule and lookup approaches demonstrate that even learned policy content can be frozen into auditable artifacts at deployment: DAIDALUS-style guidance bands are formally specified, and ACAS X uses machine-learning-optimized numerical lookup tables evaluated offline. End-to-end learning, such as deep reinforcement learning for manoeuvre generation, adapts to uncertainty patterns, but current verification methods for neural components do not meet the predictability required of a safety-adjacent gate.

The adopted architecture therefore uses a deterministic core that owns parsing, units, timeouts, state transitions, conflict thresholds and output gating. A separately monitored machine-learning inference service may contribute estimates — trajectory priors, detection confidence — only through explicit interfaces and always behind deterministic checks. Technology was selected for verification tractability, not for novelty appeal.

8.4 Prediction-to-advisory workflow

The reproducible core loop is the artefact that makes the research question testable. Note the two rejection paths: the monitor gate and the explicit no-reliable-recommendation state.

9  Human Factors and the Advisory Interface

9.1 Design question

The design question is not whether a glowing predicted path looks impressive, but whether a crew can determine at a glance what is measured, what is predicted, what is proposed, and what is broken. Published human-factors evidence constrains the answer. Cockpit traffic display interface requirements already exist as a standardized human-interface specification covering visual and aural alert characteristics and traffic display requirements. Alerting research shows measurable behavioural trade-offs: as false-alarm rates rise, pilot compliance with alerts falls, while concurrent-task performance can improve because fewer interruptions occur. Three-tier alerting showed no significant advantage over two-tier designs. Automation-assisted flying produced lower situational awareness than manual or interactive modes. Research on the ACAS X family found that the dimension and presentation of an advisory affect pilot response time, and that differing advisory formats require dedicated training. For remote operations, colour and motion cues together with labelled speed data reduce time-to-contact judgement bias, and optimized head-up display presentation reallocates attention usefully in low-visibility conditions.

9.3 The ghost path, demoted to a research visualization

The original concept described an augmented-reality windshield overlay in which the negotiated trajectory is drawn as a glowing line that the pilot follows by default, with divergence triggering alarms. That design is postponed. Version 0.1 uses a conventional advisory panel in which the proposed path is a clearly dashed line that must not resemble a flight-director command, and divergence from an advisory is an ordinary pilot act rather than an alarm condition.

The evidence in Section 9.1 predicts why. A confident overlay fed by uncertain data is the configuration that manufactures automation bias, and the compliance penalty for false alarms is measured rather than theoretical. Progression to an augmented-reality windshield is gated on controlled human-factors study results (stage V4, Section 12), because display and automation choices demonstrably change response behaviour. The first prototype displays advisory information only; separate evidence and approvals would be required before any active flight-control integration could be considered.

10.2 Cybersecurity and traffic-data integrity

The cooperative feeds on which ANIRBAN-1 depends are the most attackable links in the system, and this is measured, published ground rather than speculation. ADS-B was designed without cryptographic security mechanisms and is susceptible to message injection and sequencing manipulation. Published countermeasures trade cost against partial protection: a framework providing message authentication and integrity without depending on public-key cryptography at a bandwidth overhead below 50 per cent, and a planned aviation datalink security architecture combining national cryptographic algorithms for confidentiality and integrity with TESLA-based authentication of control data at an average byte overhead below 10 per cent. The operational threat is documented: national security authorities have reported foreign intelligence penetration of connected drone inspection platforms, with exploited weaknesses catalogued as unpatched vulnerabilities and weak credentials, ambiguous permission boundaries, unencrypted data transmission and device-firmware backdoors. Regulatory response is moving from guidance toward mandate, with a mandatory national standard on civil-UAV data-link cybersecurity in drafting that covers identity authentication, anti-forgery and anti-tampering, secure updates and anti-replay protection.

10.3 Authority boundaries and operational limitations

The pilot and air traffic control retain authority. ANIRBAN-1 output is explicitly advisory and does not change the legal or operational authority structure. Liability questions remain open and are noted rather than resolved: in concept the crew retains final authority while the system negotiates with other aircraft, and any incident investigation would examine algorithmic decisions, which is why validation evidence and complete logging (SYS-010) are treated as first-class deliverables.

Operational limitations are stated without softening. Full benefit requires equipage density, so legacy unequipped traffic does not benefit. Non-cooperative obstacle detection is limited by weather, lighting, geometry and processing. GNSS-denied conditions degrade intent sharing. Fallback to existing ATC separation and to TCAS/ACAS is therefore mandatory, not optional.

12.2 Baselines and the ablation discipline

Three baselines define what "improvement" would mean. Baseline A is constant-velocity extrapolation with a geometric proximity test. Baseline B is a DAIDALUS-style detect-and-avoid implementation: NASA’s reference logic provides a well-clear definition, look-ahead prediction of violation intervals, conflict and recovery guidance bands, and graduated alerting, and is openly available with prototype implementations and formal specifications. Baseline C is a published four-dimensional trajectory conflict-detection and resolution method whose documentation permits reproduction; polynomial-based distributed resolution minimizing deviation from original trajectories is the candidate recorded in the supplied comparison.

The ANIRBAN variant is intent sharing plus uncertainty handling plus fault-aware coordination. Ablation runs must disable one feature at a time. Without ablation, a measured improvement cannot be attributed to any component — which is precisely the standard that concept claims of this kind have so far failed to meet.

14  Discussion, Research Gap, Proposed Contributions, and Limitations

14.1 Mapping the occupied territory

Layering ANIRBAN-1 against the evidence base produces an honest overlap map. Trajectory mathematics: occupied. Conflict screening and detection research, including space-time reachability treatments of motion and pilot-intent uncertainty, and polynomial-based distributed resolution: occupied. Manoeuvre optimization including learning-based methods: occupied. Alerting and guidance logic with formal verification: occupied by DAIDALUS, and being operationally superseded by the ACAS X family. Intent sharing to ground services and between air-traffic systems: under active deployment in national TBO programmes. Air-to-air broadcast of current state: fielded through ADS-B. Air-to-air ad hoc networking services: standardized in draft form.

14.2 The surviving research gap

What remains genuinely open is a coherent, testable specification of fault-aware peer coordination: message expiry and staleness semantics; deterministic disagreement and tie-break outcomes; explicit unconfirmed states when peers do not answer; partition behaviour that preserves local detection; and an advisory interface that exposes all of this to a human. These are protocol-and-evidence problems rather than algorithm problems, which is why Sections 7 and 9 carry the novelty burden in this revision.

16  Conclusions and Next Steps

16.1 Conclusion

ANIRBAN-1 is worth building precisely because the systems around it already work. Probabilistic multi-directional collision avoidance, open well-clear alerting logic with formal specification, verified four-dimensional trajectory sharing, and standardized air-to-air ad hoc networking all exist. The open frontier is not smarter avoidance and not a new network. It is whether aircraft can share intent and coordinate under fault, uncertainty and disagreement without ever making their existing safety depend on that cooperation. That question is answerable with bench computers, simulators and honest baselines, and the scope defined in this report is sized to answer it.

Until the ablation baselines of Section 12 have been run, the correct statement of ANIRBAN-1 status is that it is a well-posed research hypothesis with a credible, low-cost test path — not a demonstrated safety improvement, and not a replacement for any existing system.
